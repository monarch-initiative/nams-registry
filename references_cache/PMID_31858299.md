# Model of Patient-Specific Immune-Enhanced Organoids for Immunotherapy Screening: Feasibility Study.
**Authors:** Votanopoulos KI, Forsythe S, Sivakumar H, Mazzocchi A, Aleman J, Miller L, Levine E, Triozzi P, Skardal A
**Journal:** Ann Surg Oncol (2020)
**DOI:** [10.1245/s10434-019-08143-8](https://doi.org/10.1245/s10434-019-08143-8)

## Content

INTRODUCTION: We hypothesized that engineering a combined lymph node/melanoma organoid from the same patient would allow tumor, stroma, and immune system to remain viable for personalized immunotherapy screening.

METHODS: Surgically obtained matched melanoma and lymph node biospecimens from the same patient were transferred to the laboratory and washed with saline, antibiotic, and red blood cell lysis buffer. Biospecimens were dissociated, incorporated into an extracellular matrix (ECM)-based hydrogel system, and biofabricated into three dimensional (3D) mixed melanoma/node organoids. Cells were not sorted, so as to preserve tumor heterogeneity, including stroma and immune cell components, resulting in immune-enhanced patient tumor organoids (iPTOs). Organoid sets were screened in parallel with nivolumab, pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, and dabrafenib/trametinib for 72 h. LIVE/DEAD staining and quantitative metabolism assays recorded relative drug efficacy. Histology and immunohistochemistry were used to compare tumor melanoma cells with organoid melanoma cells. Lastly, node-enhanced iPTOs were employed to activate patient-matched peripheral blood T cells for killing of tumor cells in naïve PTOs.

RESULTS: Ten biospecimen sets obtained from eight stage III and IV melanoma patients were reconstructed as symbiotic immune/tumor organoids between September 2017 and June 2018. Successful establishment of viable organoid sets was 90% (9/10), although organoid yield varied with biospecimen size. Average time from organoid development to initiation of immunotherapy testing was 7 days. In three patients for whom a node was not available, it was substituted with peripheral blood mononuclear cells. iPTO response to immunotherapy was similar to specimen clinical response in 85% (6/7) patients. In an additional pilot study, peripheral T cells were circulated through iPTOs, and subsequently transferred to naïve PTOs from the same patient, resulting in tumor killing, suggesting a possible role of iPTOs in generating adaptive immunity.

CONCLUSION: Development of 3D mixed immune-enhanced tumor/node organoids is a feasible platform, allowing individual patient immune system and tumor cells to remain viable for studying of personalized immunotherapy response.

Reconstructing the patient’s own tumor in the form of tumor organoids recapitulates the tumor microenvironment by incorporating tumor cells along with associated stroma and tumor infiltrating leucocytes (TILs). These tumor organoids have demonstrated promising results in predicting individual response to chemotherapy for patients with colorectal and gastroesophageal cancer, with 88% positive predictive value and 100% negative predictive value. 1

The mechanisms of action of immunotherapy drugs require an intact immune system that, with the exception of tumor-infiltrated lymphocytes (TILs), is not otherwise present in current tumor organoid reconstruction models. In addition, the degree of tumor infiltration by TILs shows significant variability between tumors, while it is not uncommon for TILs to be exhausted due to protracted exposure to the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, lack of a comprehensive immune system component within the organoids possibly limits their ability to predict response to immunotherapy. Lymph nodes are easily accessible through image-guided biopsies or part of the surgical resection field of almost every cancer for which immunotherapy is currently used. In addition, they incorporate 80% of the immune system representation of every individual patient, along with a robust population of antigen presenting cells (APCs). 2 While traditionally many types of immune cells have been difficult to maintain in vitro, this has become more feasible using bioengineered 3D culture systems, suggesting that incorporating aspects of the immune system into organoids has scientific merit. 3 We have previously developed biofabrication technologies 4- 8 to create a broad range of 3D tissues and organ-on-a-chip systems. 8- 11 Recently, we applied these experimental capabilities to produce advanced tumor models, including a tumor-on-a-chip (TOC) metastasis model and leukemia/lymphoma model. 12 , 13 To date, we have generated patient-derived tumor organoids (PTOs) from lung, mesothelioma, melanoma, colorectal, appendiceal, glioblastoma, sarcoma, and a variety of rare tumors, establishing a take rate across biospecimens of > 85%. 14- 17

Herein, we hypothesize that introducing a lymph node component or a peripheral blood T cell component into tumor organoids from the same patient will create mixed tumor/node organoids and tumor/peripheral T cell organoids, allowing for individual patient tumor and stroma and immune system to remain viable for personalized immunotherapy screening.

Ten tissue biospecimens sets were obtained from eight surgically treated patients with stage III or IV melanoma from November 2017 to December 2018 in adherence to the guidelines of the Wake Forest Baptist Medical Center institutional review board (IRB) protocols. The specimens were placed in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium and transferred fresh to the laboratory by a dedicated tissue procurement manager. Clinical information was not shared with the laboratory, with the exception of type of tumor and type of prior treatments. Once received, tissues were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 2% penicillin–streptomycin for three 5-min cycles then washed in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 2% penicillin–streptomycin for two 5-min cycles. Tissues were individually minced and placed into DMEM with 2% penicillin–streptomycin and 10% collagenase/hyaluronidase for up to 2 h on a shaker plate at 37 °C (10× collagenase/hyaluronidase in DMEM; STEMCELL Technologies, Seattle, WA). Digested tissues were then filtered through a 100-μm cell filter and centrifuged to create a cell pellet. Plasma and noncellular material was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL BD PharmLyse with 9 mL deionized water for 5 min (BD PharmLyse, San Diego, CA). The conical was filled to 50 mL with deionized water and centrifuged, lyse buffer with lysed cells was aspirated, and the cell number was counted for use. In the case of three patients, lymph node tissue was unavailable, and blood draws provided peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as an immune system substitute for enrichment of organoid formation. Blood was collected according to the same IRB protocols and under patient consent. Blood was delivered and processed within 24 h of collection. The lymphocyte cell fraction was isolated utilizing Ficoll-Paque PLUS lymphocyte isolation solution (GE Healthcare GE17-1440-02) under company instructions and resuspended in DMEM-10. The cells were then centrifuged and resuspended in 1 mL BD PharmLyse with 9 mL deionized water for 5 min (BD PharmLyse, San Diego, CA). The conical was filled to 50 mL with deionized water and centrifuged, lyse buffer with lysed cells was aspirated, and the cell number was counted for use.

An ECM-mimicking HA/collagen-based hydrogel was prepared, modified from an HA/gelatin system (HyStem-HP, ESI-BIO, Alameda, CA) that has been previously described. 8 , 18 , 19 Briefly, a thiolated HA component (Glycosil, ESI-BIO) and a methacrylated collagen component (Advanced BioMatrix, San Diego, CA) were dissolved in sterile water containing 0.05% w/v of 2-hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenone (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) as photoinitiator to make 2 and 6 mg/mL solutions, respectively. The collagen solution was then neutralized using manufacturer-provided neutralization solution at 85 μL solution per milliliter of collagen. The HA and collagen solutions were then mixed in 3:1 ratio by volume and mixed thoroughly. The resulting mixture was employed to suspend the unsorted cell populations from biospecimens at cell density of 10 million cells/mL hydrogel precursor solution. PTOs (cells supported by the ECM hydrogel) were formed by pipetting 5 μL hydrogel precursor cell suspension into 48-well plates previously coated with a thin layer of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) to form consistently round droplets, which were subsequently photo-crosslinked by ultraviolet light exposure (365 nm, 18 W cm−2) for 1 s, initiating crosslinking through thiol–methacrylate and methacrylate–methacrylate bond formation. As an optional addition, organoids could be integrated into microfluidic devices, as recently described. 9 PTOs were subsequently maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS (Hyclone, Logan, UT), 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 1% l-glutamine. For immune-enhanced PTOs (iPTOs), the whole population of cells derived from a lymph node from one patient was used to supplement the cells from the tumor biospecimen. In these organoids, the lymph node cells were added at 1:1 ratio to the tumor cells.

LIVE/DEAD staining (LIVE/DEAD viability/cytotoxicity kit for mammalian cells; Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) was performed on day 7 for initial assessment and at the end of the drug exposure period (4 days in total). Spent medium was first aspirated from wells, after which 100 μL of mixture of PBS and DMEM (1:1) containing 2 μM calcein-AM and 2 μM ethidium homodimer-1 was introduced. Constructs were incubated for 60 min, after which spent medium was again aspirated and replaced with clean PBS. Fluorescent imaging was performed using a Leica TCS LSI macro confocal microscope. z-Stacks (100 μm) were obtained for each construct using filters appropriate for both red and green fluorescence (594 and 488 nm, respectively) then overlaid.

On day 10, PTOs and iPTOs were washed twice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, then additionally washed twice with DPBS. Cultures were histologically processed, paraffin embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Tissue sections on microscope slides were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to visualize biomarkers S100, KI67, CD4+, CD3+, CD8+, and granzyme B. Blocking was performed by incubation under Dako Protein Block for 15 min. Primary antibodies S100 (ab52642, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/200 dilution), tyrosinase (ab170905, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/200 dilution), MelanA (ab210546, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/1000 dilution), KI67 (ab16667, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/100 dilution), CD4+ (ab133616, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/500 dilution), CD8+ (ab4055, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/200 dilution), or granzyme B (ab4059, abcam, raised in rabbit, 1/100 dilution) were applied to the sections on the slides in Dako Antibody Diluent and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. For S100, tyrosinase, MelanA, and KI67, chromogenic staining was performed. A biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (BP-9100, Vector Laboratories, 1/200 dilution) was applied and incubated for 1 h, after which a streptavidin-functionalized ABC Elite reagent was introduced. Finally, 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) was added and incubated for approximately 30 s to visualize the stain. For CD4+, CD8+, and granzyme B, secondary Alexa Fluor 594 antibodies (Thermo Fisher) with appropriate species reactivity were applied to all samples at 1:200 in Dako Antibody Diluent and left at room temperature for 1 h (anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, A-11070). Sections were then incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min prior to cover-slipping. Sections were imaged on a Leica upright brightfield and fluorescent microscope.

Drug screening experiments typically initiated on day 7 following PTO and iPTO biofabrication and involved administering the following compounds to the organoids and incubating under these conditions for 72 h. Each organoid set’s group of drug compounds varied slightly due to the PTO and iPTO yield from each particular set of biospecimens. However, each drug screen evaluated the programmed cell death protein (PD)-1 inhibitors pembrolizumab and nivolumab and the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein (CTLA)-4 inhibitor ipilimumab. In cases where organoid numbers were larger, the cocktail of dabrafenib and trametinib as well as cisplatin were chosen for evaluation, representing therapies with different mechanisms of action. Concentrations for each treatment as follows were based on preliminary studies: pembrolizumab (100 nM), nivolumab (100 nM), ipilimumab (100 nM), and dabrafenib (40 μM), and trametinib (2 μM).

LIVE/DEAD staining was performed on the third day following initiation of drug incubation, as described above. Relative mitochondrial metabolism was quantified as an indicator of cellular response to drug treatments by adenosine triphosphate (ATP) activity quantification assays. ATP assays (CellTiter Glo 3D; Promega, Madison, WI) determined relative cell number by quantification of cellular ATP. Luminescence values were determined on a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader M5 (Thermo Fisher) tunable plate reader system.

iPTOs and PTOs were prepared from a single melanoma biospecimen and lymph node specimen from the same patient as described above. The white blood cell fraction from a blood draw from the patient was processed as described above. This cell suspension was introduced to the iPTOs and maintained in culture for 7 days, after which the supernatant was collected and transferred to naïve PTOs in 24-well plates. After 7 additional days of incubation, viability was assessed visually by LIVE/DEAD staining and imaging.

Data are expressed for each experimental group as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and statistical significance determined using statistical analysis methods (GraphPad Prism, Graphpad Software Inc., USA). An n = 3 or higher was employed for all studies. Histological, immunohistochemical, or fluorescent images presented in figures are representative of their respective experimental groups. Student’s t-tests were performed to compare the means of a normally distributed interval dependent variable for two independent groups. Confidence intervals of 95% or better were considered significant.

Tumor and lymph node tissues were transported to the laboratory generally within 1 h of resection, after which enzymatic tissue dissociation lasted between 1 and 2 h. Alternatively, a biospecimen would be placed in RPMI media and stored at 4 °C overnight prior to processing. In these cases, no decrease in cell viability or cell yield was observed. Biofabrication of PTOs and iPTOs using the supportive ECM hydrogel biomaterials (Fig. 1a) required an additional 30–60 min following dissociation, depending on cell yield, which determined the number of total organoids fabricated. From the biospecimens received in this study, 75–100 organoids were generated in each instance, typically split into equal numbers of PTOs and iPTOs.

Based on our previously described experience in screening PTOs from other tumor types, 14 , 16 , 20 we hypothesized that we could capture checkpoint inhibitor responses by incorporating patients’ own nodal tissue into the organoid constructs. In our first attempt, PTOs and node-enhanced PTOs were formed from a melanoma biospecimen. After verifying organoid viability, on day 7 the organoids were treated with the PD-1 inhibitors pembrolizumab or nivolumab for 72 h. LIVE/DEAD staining and fluorescent imaging were used to evaluate drug response. Interestingly, the PD-1 inhibitors resulted in a clear increase in overall melanoma cell death in the node-enhanced organoids but not in the organoids that were lacking the lymph node component (Fig. 1b). These results provided positive confirmation of the feasibility of our hypothesis and led to subsequent additional studies employing iPTOs for checkpoint inhibitor drug screening.

Organoids were initially characterized through a set of staining protocols. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed intact cell nuclei inside relatively small cells, located uniformly throughout the organoids (Fig. 2a). LIVE/DEAD staining showed that, after 7 days in culture, the organoids comprised predominantly viable cells (Fig. 2b), indicating our capability of maintaining them in vitro. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) using chromogenic staining was employed to identify specific biomarkers in the organoids. Specifically, the melanoma biomarkers S100 beta, tyrosinase, and melanA were employed to verify that the organoids maintained a melanoma-specific phenotype (Fig. 2c-e), while the proliferation marker Ki-67 was used to demonstrate cellular proliferation in the organoids (Fig. 2f).

Immune-component presence and functionality were assessed and confirmed using a panel of IHC biomarkers following checkpoint inhibitor treatment (Fig. 2g) of one set of iPTOs. Specifically, iPTOs were treated with ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, or nivolumab for 72 h, after which iPTOs that responded to treatment were processed for IHC. First, immunofluorescence confirmed the presence of both CD4+ helper T cells (Fig. 2h) and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 2i), verifying that iPTOs maintain important components of the originating patients’ immune system, as hypothesized. Importantly, staining for granzyme B was positive, indicating successful activation and tumor cell killing activity in the iPTOs—in this specific case, an iPTO response to the CTLA-4 inhibitor ipilimumab (Fig. 2j).

To more comprehensively evaluate our iPTO platform, and to assess its clinical correlation, six additional pairs of patient-matched melanoma and immune-component biospecimens were employed to assess checkpoint inhibitor responses in corresponding sets of iPTOs versus PTOs. After 7 days in culture, viability was verified as described in Fig. 2b, after which organoids were treated with pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, and dabrafenib/trametinib cocktail regardless of the BRAF status of the tumor.

As seen in clinical practice, we observed a separation of datasets in terms of responders and nonresponders to the therapies. Three patient sets of PTOs and iPTOs showed almost no decrease in ATP activity compared with no-treatment control groups (Fig. 3a-c). These quantitative ATP-based results are largely corroborated by LIVE/DEAD staining and macro-confocal microscopy-based fluorescent images showing that, while there were dead cells intermixed throughout the organoids, they were generally low in number compared with viable cells (Fig. 3d). Perhaps more importantly, there was very little difference between treatment groups regardless of being PTOs or iPTOs.

Conversely, the other three patient sets of PTOs and iPTOs showed statistically significant differences for specific therapies. One patient set of organoids showed significant decreases in ATP activity (generally proportional to cell number and overall viability) in iPTOs for all three checkpoint inhibitor treatments, while non-immune-enhanced PTO ATP activity was approximately equivalent to control levels (Fig. 3e). This set of organoids did not respond to dabrafenib and trametinib, regardless of immune enhancement. Another patient set of organoids also showed statistically significant decreases in ATP activity in response to checkpoint inhibitor treatments when immune enhanced (Fig. 3f). A third set of organoids also showed a response only to ipilimumab (Fig. 3g). However, note that the ATP data appear to indicate that perhaps there was proliferation or increased metabolic activity in some of the organoids but not others, potentially alluding to clonal differences. The outcome of the second responder (Fig. 3f) was corroborated by LIVE/DEAD staining and macro-confocal-based fluorescent images showing that checkpoint inhibitor-treated groups of iPTOs had more dead cells than PTOs (Fig. 3h).

Melanoma specimens were obtained from patients with either stage III (30%, 3/10) or stage IV (60%, 6/10) who required surgery for local control. The responses of iPTOs to immunotherapy were compared with the corresponding patients’ clinical response. Clinical data were available for 78% (7/9) of the generated organoids. The remaining 22% (2/9) of the organoid sets had no available correlative data to checkpoint inhibitor (CPI) response for the following reasons: no residual disease post resection for a stage IIIB patient and denial of immunotherapy for a stage IV patient. Organoids were not generated from a devitalized by electrocoagulation set of specimens (10%, 1/10, patient 4017029). Six of seven (85%7) iPTOs with available correlative data recapitulated the clinical response of the resected specimens. One iPTO set (15%) failed to correlate with the patient’s response. More specifically, the patient’s iPTO was responsive to nivolumab while the corresponding patient progressed at 2.5 months while being treated with nivolumab (patient 4017032, Table 1).

Two out of eight (25%) patients who progressed while on treatment with CPIs had their progressing lesions resected and reconstructed as iPTOs. These organoids exhibited a response that correlated with the clinical response of the resected specimens. More specifically:

iPTOs from a BRAF wild-type patient showed a nonanticipated 60% reduction in melanoma viability by dabrafenib and trametinib (D/T), but no response to CPIs (Fig. 4a). At that time, the response to D/T was considered to be a false-positive result given the BRAF wild-type status of the patient. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) performed after clinical progression on CPIs demonstrated a downstream MEK mutation that was bypassing the BRAF pathway. Based on the NGS results, the patient was treated with trametinib and responded. After initial response to trametinib, the patient developed resistance (Fig. 4b). A nonresponding to trametinib lesion was resected and reconstructed into organoids, showing no further response to D/T and thus recapitulating the clinical behavior of the lesion (Fig. 4a, b, patient 4017010, Table 1).

iPTOs from a stage IIIB melanoma patient demonstrated no response to CPIs or D/T despite presence of BRAF V600E mutation (Fig. 3b, d, patient 4017025, Table 1). The patient developed local recurrence while on nivolumab. Organoids developed from the resected local recurrence recapitulated the clinical response of the patient. The patient has not been treated with D/T, therefore correlation for these drugs is not available.

Finally, in a pilot study, node-enhanced iPTOs were explored for induction of adaptive immunity. These patient-matched peripheral blood T cells were exposed to node-enhanced PTOs for 7 days in the hope that colocalization of melanoma neoantigens and APCs would generate T cells with tumor-specific antineoplastic memory (Fig. 4c). After day 7, the supernatant was collected, containing cells in suspension, and administered to tumor-only PTOs (Fig. 4d) prepared from the same tumor biospecimen from which the iPTOs had been prepared. On day 1, naïve PTOs showed high viability, visualized via LIVE/DEAD staining (Fig. 4e), as did iPTOs on day 7 (Fig. 4f). After 7 days of circulation through iPTOs, the supernatant containing T cells (and other immune cells) was transferred to the tumor-only organoids and incubated for another 7 days, after which LIVE/DEAD imaging showed a high degree of cell death, suggesting T cell-mediated tumor cell killing. Note that we do not believe this cell killing to be spontaneous cell death due to the length in culture, as we have maintained organoids from a variety of tumor types in culture for much longer than 2 weeks with high viability.

Checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs) have been welcomed as a big step forward in the management of metastatic melanoma. Nevertheless, the vast majority of patients treated with CPIs will not respond, as indicated by the fact that 79% of metastatic melanoma patients treated with ipilimumab die within 5 years, while combination therapy with ipilimumab plus nivolumab results in median progression-free survival of 11.5 months as opposed to 2.9 months with ipilimumab monotherapy. 21 , 22 Despite advances in immunotherapy, currently there is no 100% human experimental model to recapitulate the interaction between host, tumor, and immune system. It is currently unknown which patients will respond to immunotherapy. Here, we hypothesized that constructing and introducing a lymph node organoid into tumor organoids from the same patient would create a mixed tumor/node organoid, allowing for individual patient tumor and stroma and immune system to remain viable for personalized immunotherapy screening.

We selected lymph nodes as an integral component of surgical resection that incorporates a robust population of APCs. Therefore, nodal tissue that otherwise would decay in pathology departments can be mobilized to support diagnostic and treatment needs. Combining PTOs with node-derived cells brings into contact patient-specific tumor cells with major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and class II-proficient nodal cells, allowing for cross-presentation of antigens through the abundance of patient-specific APCs incorporated into the immune-enhanced PTOs. This yields an experimental model that faithfully mimics the interaction between the patient, tumor, and immune system while allowing for real-time measurement of immune activation and immune-mediated cytotoxic activity by sampling of its supportive medium.

In the present study, the organoid take rate was 90% (9/10) within 24 h from tissue procurement. As a contrast, attempts to recapitulate the tumor microenvironment in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models produces results in 20% of patients approximately 6 months later when a patient with metastatic disease has either succumbed to the disease or clinical decisions have already been made.

When the iPTO response was compared with the clinical response of the patient to immunotherapy, they correlated in 85% (6/7). All six patients whose organoids did not respond to immunotherapy had progression of disease while on treatment. Two of the patients who experienced recurrence while on treatment had a second set of organoids built directly from the recurrences that recapitulated the clinical response of the recurrence. This raises the obvious question of whether management of recurrences can be approached through organoid-guided personalized selection of available chemotherapy and immunotherapy rather than through nonpersonalized outcomes generated by clinical trials. It also provides a window to study treatment failure as a function of coexisting tumor clones with different cell population and possibly different biologic behavior.

In addition, the response of the organoid represents the response of the corresponding component of the biopsied tumor. In advanced stages of disease, tumors may have developed clonality through escape mutations that will not be captured by a single-site biopsy. These nonresponding clones will eventually determine the fate of the patient, not the clones that respond to treatment. Currently, with the available technology, we cannot accurately map clonality prior to treatment. However, we can biopsy a recurrence and grow organoids from recurrent or progressing sites and study their response, as was done for two patients in this study.

The advantages of testing a construct that incorporates the entire genome of the patient’s tumor was exhibited when a wild-type BRAF patient had organoids responding to a MEK inhibitor. Next-generation sequencing of the patient’s tumor demonstrated a MEK mutation that was bypassing the BRAF pathway. This mutation was identified by the organoids prior to NGS. It is possible that organoids have the potential to provide empirical information on drug efficacy on pathways that, despite being present and activated in the tumor genome, are currently understudied or yet unknown.

In cases where lymph node is not part of the surgical specimens, we routinely use PBMCs as an immune-component substitute. In our experience with a variety of tumors, this is a viable alternative in creating immune-enhanced organoids. Currently, both constructs are developed in parallel to study their relative efficacy in predicting immune response. We believe that the major advantage that symbiotic tumor/node organoids offer as an experimental platform over tumor/PBMN organoids is their significant number of mature APCs.

The major limitation of this study is the modest number of patients. Nevertheless, it is a feasibility study that accurately describes the conceptual framework for application of tumor/node symbiotic organoids in personalized immunotherapy screening. Verification in a larger cohort of patients is currently underway, with inclusion of patients from different primaries. Also of interest is the observation of an apparent difference in response to pembrolizumab and nivolumab—at least when evaluating visual LIVE/DEAD assays. In unpublished studies using other tumor types, we have seen even more pronounced differences. While these therapies target the same receptor interactions, this suggests that perhaps the two antibodies have slightly differing affinities, resulting in different efficacies. It should be noted that this is speculative and requires further research to be validated.

Finally, we present pilot data in which the peripheral white blood cell fraction of the patient was circulated through tumor/node symbiotic organoids, attempting to generate adaptive immunity against the patient’s own melanoma organoids. These data presented herein require additional exploration with more patients but suggest significant versatility in applications utilizing immune system components in an ex vivo organoid setting. Similarly, Voest et al. recently generated tumor-reactive T cells by coculturing them with PTOs from 13 mismatch repair (MMR)-deficient colon and 6 non-small-cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC) patients. 23

Creation of immune-enhanced tumor organoids in a symbiotic state with patient’s own lymph nodes is feasible, resulting in a 100% human experimental platform that recapitulates the interaction between host, tumor, and immune system within 24 h of tissue procurement. Moreover, this platform and others like it offer unique opportunities for therapy screening and exploration of novel treatment concepts that may have potential for clinical utility in the future.
